🔒 SECURITY TIPS
X
⚠ Power from a wall charger
Power AutoFOV from a USB wall charger — not a computer. A USB data link to a PC can expose the device console and allow reflashing.
Use a strong login password
Set a unique device password in WiFi Setup. It guards the dashboard and firmware flashing. Blank falls back to the random code shown on the device's screen during setup.
Keep AutoFOV on your LAN
Traffic is plain HTTP (no TLS, by design). Never port-forward AutoFOV to the internet or join it to an untrusted public network.
Prefer the IP over autofov.local
On a shared network the device’s numeric IP address skips the mDNS name lookup, which other devices can spoof. Otherwise the two are equally secure.
Sign out on shared computers
Your login is remembered per-browser. Tap SIGN OUT when you’re on a machine that isn’t yours.
Locked out? Hold BOOT
Hold the BOOT button while powering on to relaunch WiFi Setup and set new credentials — no calibration is lost.
Can’t update?
If an update fails or leaves AutoFOV misbehaving, you can reflash over USB from a browser — no terminal needed, and it keeps your calibration and WiFi settings. (Bookmark the link now — you can’t reach it from a dead device.)

HOW TO CALIBRATE
X
What it does
AutoFOV measures the bellows separation distance and converts it to a horizontal field-of-view. Calibration fits that distance→FOV curve to YOUR bellows and camera from a few points.
It does not account for post-process / stack-program cropping. If your shot isn’t on the optic axis, or you crop in post, derive the real FOV as
(cropped ÷ uncropped horizontal px) × the stack’s AVG FOV.
You'll need
A stage micrometer (a ruler of known scale) in the frame, so the pixel width of a known distance can be measured at each step.
The settings (tap a row to type)
Photo Width — your camera's image width in px (e.g. 6960).
Demarc Dist — the real width (mm) you measure pixels across (default 0.40).
Tick Pitch — your micrometer's smallest tick spacing (mm, default 0.010). Used by MEASURE FROM PHOTO; set it here, before starting.
Cal Points — a TARGET step count; you don't have to hit it exactly.
Steps
1. Set Photo Width, Demarc Dist & Tick Pitch, then START CAL.
2. At each bellows distance, fill in the pixel count — either type it, or tap 📷 MEASURE FROM PHOTO and drop a micrometer JPEG to measure it automatically.
3. Tap CAPTURE & SAVE (pairs your pixels with the live ToF distance).
4. Move the bellows ~10 mm and repeat across its full range.
Measure from photo
Drop a JPEG of the micrometer; it finds the ticks, deskews any tilt, and fills the pixel field. It refuses to apply a result it can't trust (too few ticks / high error) — retake or check the Tick Pitch setting if so. Shoot JPEG (or RAW+JPEG); a full uncropped frame.
Stop anytime · review
Capture points at whatever spacing you manage, then FINISH to fit from exactly the points you took. Use 3+ (5+ across the whole range is better). REVIEW lets you check or RETAKE any point. CAL I/O backs up / restores / shares a calibration.
◎ LENS CENTERING
?
X
What it does
Finds where the lens’s red/blue lateral color fringing crosses zero — the true optical axis — and compares it to the frame center. If they differ, the lens/sensor is shifted left/right.

What to drop
The whole, uncropped micrometer .jpg series — the same calibration set. It needs the full magnification sweep (zoomed-in through zoomed-out): 6+ frames spanning ≥~1.4×. A short or one-sided sweep reads INCONCLUSIVE — the per-frame neutral point drifts with magnification and only a balanced sweep averages that out.

Reading it
WELL-CENTERED ✓ — neutral point within ±1% of center.
OFF-AXIS ⚠ — axis offset by the shown amount; center X% is how far off (% of half-field), ±Y% the uncertainty.
INCONCLUSIVE — need a fuller sweep, or the offset isn’t resolved from noise.
CA avg/max µm is the lateral-color magnitude out at the edges.

Tip
This checks the left/right axis only. Rotate the micrometer 90° and reshoot to test top/bottom. (tap anywhere to close)

What this is
A passive vibration monitor (the onboard accelerometer). It watches how much the rig shakes and how long it takes to settle after a movement or the shutter — it never delays or gates capture, it only informs. The CALM / … badge is the overall level right now.

Live readouts
DOMINANT FREQUENCY — the single loudest frequency at this instant, split into V (vertical — along the optical axis, the motion that actually blurs a focus stack) and H (horizontal, across the plane). 10 s PEAK is the worst seen in the last 10 s (peak-hold) — it catches brief bumps like a footstep or a door. The mg / µm button toggles units: acceleration (mg) vs displacement (µm, how far it actually moves).

Signatures
Name and CAPTURE the current vibration as a fingerprint, so you can recognise a recurring source later (AC unit, pump, footsteps) by matching its spectrum.

Wait analysis
After a shutter pulse it fits the resonance ring-down and shows a RECOMMENDED SETTLE time. YOUR WAIT is what’s configured on the controller now (edit it to change). RE-LOCK re-measures & locks the table’s resonance frequency used for that estimate; SETTLE PLOT ▶ opens the per-stack settle-time history.

Analyzer
ANALYZE ▶ (or tapping the spectrum) opens the full multi-pane view: live spectrum, a scrolling waterfall, and the RMS trend.

Tip
For focus stacking the V channel matters most — that’s the axis that shifts focus. Let the numbers settle for a few seconds before trusting a reading. (tap anywhere here to close)

STACK VIBRATION REPORT ? no stack recorded yet ✕
What it does
Every shutter pulse of the last stack is scored for during-exposure blur (stage motion, V and H, same math as the analyzer's broadband row) so the shakiest frames can be culled — with 80%+ overlap, dropping a frame costs nothing. The list is ranked worst-first; rows above the threshold are tinted red.

Columns
BLUR V/H µm — stage movement during that frame's exposure window. PX EQUIV — the worse axis converted to camera pixels at the subject (µm/px from the stack's FOV ÷ photo width): the number that decides visible softness. SETTLE ms — pre-shot ring-down at the locked resonance, 77 ms steps. — means no clean decay was found in the 2.5 s window — normal on a quiet bench (flat noise never “decays”); suspect it only when blur is high too.

Threshold
Frames blurred beyond N camera pixels are flagged; drag the slider and the list, ticks and count update live. The default is the optical spot itself (computed from the MTF tool's eff NA and your Photo Width; 2 px when no NA is set) — once you pick a value yourself it sticks. Different camera? Set Photo Width via CALIBRATOR → PICK (camera list or megapixel calculator). Why the spot is the right anchor:

The measured band — and the ⚠ LOW-FREQ warning
Blur numbers cover 3.25–208 Hz — resonance ring-down, motor hum, footsteps' fast content. Slower motion (<3 Hz rocking/sway) is excluded: it produces almost no acceleration (a = ω²x), and its photo damage is a per-frame velocity lottery — the 8 ms exposure lands at a swing extreme (frozen) or mid-swing (smeared), which no time-averaged metric can rank (measured: 16–34 px streaks on frames predicting ≤3 px). When the report detects large sub-band displacement it shows the ⚠ LOW-FREQ MOTION banner: treat that stack's per-frame blur as unreliable and fix the slow motion mechanically (isolate the bench, stop leaning on it).

Why small blur is invisible — the optical spot
A lens can't focus a point to a point: the smallest detail an objective can render at the subject is a disc ≈0.61λ/NA across (≈0.8 µm for NA 0.42 in green). Every point in every photo is smeared into at least that disc — it's the sharpest the system can physically be. The sensor usually samples far finer than that (e.g. 0.22 µm/px under an 0.8 µm spot → the spot is already ≈3.6 px wide in every frame), so “pixels of blur” overstates small numbers. Blurs combine like the sides of a right triangle: total ≈ √(spot² + motion²) — motion below the spot fattens the total only a few percent (invisible); motion equal to the spot makes it 41% softer (noticeable); motion at twice the spot dominates (obviously soft). Think of drawing with a fat marker: wiggles smaller than the tip width don't change the line. In general the invisibility floor is whichever is larger — the optical spot or ≈1 pixel at the subject — which is also why large-pixel cameras tolerate more vibration than high-MP ones: same µm of shake, coarser ruler. (Validated on this rig: sub-spot predictions produced no measurable softening; a motor ON/OFF test separated blurred from clean frames 25/25 with rank correlation r = 0.62, and measured streak extents landed inside the predicted envelope.)

Timeline
V / H RMS envelope across the whole stack (same colors as the analyzer), one entry per FFT hop. Ticks along the bottom are frames — tall red = flagged. Hover for a time cursor; click a table row (or the strip) to pin a gold marker — the translucent gold band is the history window that frame's blur was measured from. The µm / mg toggle switches the curves between stage displacement (µm, per-frequency 1/f² integration — comparable to the blur columns; the default) and raw acceleration (mg): the two genuinely differ, µm emphasizes slow motion, mg emphasizes buzz. A strip spike with LOW blur on the nearby frame is normal, not a miss: blur measures displacement during the shutter — high frequencies shrink by 1/f² and slow sway barely moves within a 1/125 s exposure. Spikes between exposures (the stage move itself) don't blur anything.

Buttons
COPY CULL LIST — flagged frames + a bare comma list for batch tools. SAVE PNG — the stack-summary card (same as the stack-done popup), available any time. SAVED — the last 8 completed stacks, archived in this browser: they survive new stacks, page reloads and device resets (the device itself only holds the last stack, in volatile memory); CLEAR wipes the archive (two clicks). A stray test shot can't overwrite any of it. VALIDATE… — ground-truth check: drop this stack's photos (JPG/PNG, filename order = capture order) and each is sharpness-scored; the SHARP / SOFT-vs-NBRS columns appear and the status line shows the rank correlation between predicted blur and measured softness (overall + per axis). Scoring uses a native-resolution center crop (1600²) — keep the subject/target near the frame center. A flat static target — USAF-1951 is ideal, edges in both axes — removes the focus-drift confound; for a real stack the vs-neighbors metric compensates for the moving focus plane. MTF / RESOLUTION
?
CLOSE ✕
What it does
Measures the real-world resolving power of the whole imaging train — objective, relay/tube optics and camera together — from a photo of a USAF-1951 target, via the slanted-edge method. It plots contrast vs. spatial frequency (the MTF curve) against the diffraction-limited ideal for your objective and boils it down to MTF50 / MTF10 / MTF5 and the equivalent NA the system actually delivers — how much of the objective’s theoretical resolution survives the rest of the stack.

Shooting
Photograph a USAF-1951 target: pick an element that fills most of the frame and keep it near the frame center — edge/periphery elements read lower from off-axis aberrations and under-report the lens. Tilt the target ~5° so the edges are slanted, best focus, lowest ISO. Shoot RAW — in-camera JPG smooths & sharpens artificially, skewing the measured edge — and convert to a baseline TIFF.
bars fill the frame · tilt ≈5° (either direction) · drag the green box across one whole bar
Inputs
Grp/Elem — the element you photographed; its known bar pitch calibrates µm/px automatically (needs ≥2 bars in frame). eff NA — the effective NA of the whole imaging train (objective stopped by relay/tube optics); the grey ideal curve. The blue curve and the plot range use the active objective’s nominal NA from the main screen.

Measuring
Drop one or several crops (JPG/PNG/baseline TIFF) — each is focus-scored, the sharpest is pre-selected (★) and the green heatmap shows where the shot is sharpest; the box is pre-placed there. Drag the box across one whole bar — both edges are measured and averaged — or onto a single edge. Avoid bar ends/corners. Re-measure after moving the box; results are placement-independent.

Reading the plot
MTF50 — perceived-sharpness benchmark. MTF10 — practical resolution limit (≈Rayleigh 9% contrast); min sep µm = 1000/MTF10. MTF5 — approximate by-eye extinction (≈5% contrast, invisible below). The measured noise floor (read beyond the objective’s cutoff, where true MTF is zero) is power-subtracted before the markers are placed — without it MTF10/MTF5 read the noise as resolution. In-camera JPEG sharpening can still inflate the tail: for honest numbers shoot RAW→unsharpened TIFF or set sharpening to 0. equiv NA — the diffraction-limited NA whose ideal curve best fits the measurement (green dashed): the system “performs like” this NA. It is a lower bound on the true aperture (aberrations/defocus lower it) and is the right number to type into eff NA. The lp/mm ↔ µm button switches the axis to minimum resolvable separation (1000/f µm = one line-pair period ≈ Rayleigh 0.61λ/NA — the same convention as objective-datasheet “resolving power”, e.g. 0.7 µm for a 20x NA 0.42). A ⚠ warning means MTF10 exceeds the objective’s diffraction limit — physically impossible, so check Grp/Elem and the selected objective.

Trusting the numbers
The last stretch before the cutoff usually wiggles and rides a little high — residual noise the flat floor subtraction can’t fully remove (the floor tilts up with frequency). Treat everything at and beyond MTF5 as noise, not resolution. Diffraction-shaped blur gives MTF10 ≈ 2× MTF50; a clearly larger ratio — the curve steeper than the green fit through the mid-band, then above it in the tail — is the defocus fingerprint (spurious resolution). On such shots only MTF50 is a fair blur comparison; record equiv NA and MTF10/MTF5 from best-focus frames only. A lopsided 2 edges (a/b) split means focus tilts across the bar — the two edges sit a bar-width apart on the target — so re-measure a single edge on the sharpest heatmap area.

Tips
Measure both horizontal and vertical bars — a large difference indicates astigmatism or focus-plane tilt. Drag the legend if it covers the curve. (click anywhere here to close)

What this does
It finds the micrometer's tick marks, fits their spacing, and converts that to the pixel count across your Demarc Dist — the number CAPTURE & SAVE needs.
Which span should I pick?
Normally the starred one. Every option measures the same quantity — pixels per Demarc Dist — just over a different number of ticks, then rescales. So the span you measure need not match the span you calibrate across.
Longer is usually better. A wide window averages more of the frame, and the frame-average scale is exactly what the firmware assumes when it computes FOV = demarc × width ÷ pixels. On the reference photos the longest span is about 10× more certain than the 0.40 mm one.
A shorter span is the risky one. It scales a local pitch up, so any local scale error is multiplied too. It only gets recommended when it is clearly better — usually because part of the frame is soft or obstructed and it steps around the bad patch. You can always pick any option by hand.
What ± means
Three things added together:
fit noise — the scatter of the tick fit itself. Always small.
window drift — how much the answer changes as the window slides along the frame. This is real: your lens's scale drifts slightly across the field.
vs whole frame — how far this window's answer sits from a fit over every tick.
± is an estimate, with slop of its own. Treat a few-percent difference between two options as a tie, not a winner.
The tilt reading
If the slide is not square to the optic axis, focus sweeps across the frame and one end of the span goes soft. The tool measures that angle from how tick contrast falls off. It cannot tell you which way to tilt — blur looks the same above and below focus — so adjust either way and re-shoot: if the angle grows, go back the other way. (click anywhere to close)

FOCUS / TILT
?
CLOSE ✕
What it does
Drop the micrometer shots you are choosing between. For each one it measures how the tick contrast varies across the frame. A slide that is square to the optic axis is uniformly sharp; a tilted one sweeps through focus, so contrast peaks somewhere and falls away either side. Fitting that curve gives the tilt angle and the width of the in-focus band.
Reading it
Δz across the span is how far the slide climbs, in microns, over the width you calibrate across. Compare it to the depth of field: if Δz is larger, no single shot can hold the whole span in focus, and the fix is mechanical — level the stage.
Which way do I tilt?
Unknowable from a single photo: blur is symmetric about focus, so a soft left edge could be high or low. Nudge it either way and re-shoot — if the angle grows, go the other way.
The span table
Nothing requires the span you MEASURE to equal the span you CALIBRATE across. Every option fits the tick pitch over its own window and rescales it to the Demarc Dist — so a longer span scales down rather than up, and is usually the best choice: it averages more of the frame, which is exactly the frame-average scale the firmware's FOV = demarc × width ÷ px assumes. On this repo's reference frames the longest span beats the demarc span by roughly 10× on total error.
What ± is made of
fit noise — the standard error of the pitch fit. Always tiny; never the real story.
window drift — how far the answer moves as the window slides along the frame. Honest for a short window; but it shrinks toward zero for a long one simply because a long window cannot move, which is not the same as being right.
vs whole frame — how far this window's answer sits from a fit over every tick in the frame. This one does not vanish with length, and it is what "window drift" alone misses.
Treat ± as an estimate with real slop of its own — a few-percent gap between two options is a tie, not a winner. That is why a shorter span (the only kind that extrapolates a local pitch upward, multiplying any local scale error with it) must beat the demarc span by ≥25% before it is recommended — which happens when part of the frame is soft or obstructed and the short window steps around it. Longer spans face no such test; they simply compete on error. You can always pick any option by hand.

How the analyzer derives its axes
Gravity-referenced, not chip-referenced: the DSP finds “down” by itself, so mounting angle never matters.
world axes come from ĝ alone — vertical = the gravity line · horizontal = the one plane ⊥ it · chip Xc/Yc/Zc never used downstream
One plane, not two
ĝ is a slow per-axis EMA of the raw accel (α = 0.002 @ 416 Hz, τ ≈ 1.2 s): it follows tilt, ignores vibration. (At rest the chip reads +1 g along “up” — the sign never matters, ĝ marks the vertical line.) V = AC · ĝ — signed motion along that line, the axis that shifts focus → its own 512-pt FFT. H is everything left over and lives in ONE plane ⊥ ĝ: its in-plane coordinates (hx, hy) on e1/e2 are packed into one complex FFT (hx real, hy imag) whose magnitude is the single H trace.

What H can — and can’t — tell you
The rotation of e1/e2 inside the plane is arbitrary and the combined power doesn’t depend on it: H shows how much horizontal energy at each frequency, never front–back vs left–right. V is the only physically distinguished direction, because gravity marks it. Gravity is projected out first, so both channels stay linear — no √(g²+δ²) squaring artifact.

Basis stability
r̂ = chip-Z, switching to chip-X when gravity sits near chip-Z (|gz| > 0.9, released at 0.85 — hysteresis so micro-dither can’t flip the basis between FFTs; with gravity living on chip-X here, the default chip-Z reference stays active); the basis then stays frozen until the rig actually tilts ≈2°, so every FFT window sees one consistent frame.
